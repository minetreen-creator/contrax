Winning government contracts is less about writing the most impressive-sounding proposal and more about making the evaluator’s job easy. A strong response is organized around the solicitation’s instructions, evaluation criteria, and evidence. These five proposal writing practices make that discipline repeatable.

## 1. Read the RFP like an evaluator

Start with the table of contents, then read the instructions to offerors and evaluation factors before drafting. Section L tells you how to respond; Section M tells you how the response will be scored. Mark every page limit, font rule, submission instruction, and required form. Build an annotated outline that mirrors the evaluation factors.

Do not assume the statement of work is the scoring rubric. Quote the requirement you are answering and connect your approach to the factor that earns points.

## 2. Build a compliance matrix

Create a spreadsheet with columns for solicitation reference, requirement, proposal location, owner, evidence, and status. Include every “shall,” required attachment, and contract clause that affects your response. Add page counts and cross-references as the draft develops.

A compliance matrix catches omissions early and gives the team a single source of truth. It also protects against a common failure: answering a requirement somewhere in the proposal but making it impossible to find.

## 3. Prove past performance with outcomes

Choose relevant examples, not merely prestigious logos. For each project, state the customer, scope, size, period, your role, and measurable result. Explain why the example is similar to this requirement. “Delivered excellent service” is weak; cycle-time reduction, on-time milestones, acceptance rates, and documented customer outcomes are evidence.

If you lack direct past performance, be candid and show transferable experience, key personnel qualifications, a realistic transition plan, and the controls that reduce performance risk.

## 4. Make pricing support the strategy

Price to the actual work breakdown, labor categories, period of performance, materials, travel, and indirect costs. Check that the technical volume and price volume describe the same staffing and assumptions. A low price that cannot support the promised approach creates risk; a high price without a clear value case loses competitiveness.

Document assumptions and identify options where allowed. Never trade compliance for an attractive number.

## 5. Schedule a real red-team review

A red team should read the proposal as a skeptical evaluator who has never heard your pitch. Give reviewers the RFP, evaluation criteria, and a scoring sheet—not a tour from the writing team. Ask: Is every requirement answered? Is the proof specific? Can I find it quickly? Are claims supported?

Reserve time to resolve findings, proofread, validate files, and perform a final submission check. A polished proposal submitted late or in the wrong format is still a losing response. Strong proposal writing is a process: interpret, map, prove, test, and submit exactly as instructed.
