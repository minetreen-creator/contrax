SDVOSB government contracts can give veteran-owned small businesses a focused path into federal procurement. The program is more than a badge on a capability statement: it can determine whether your company is eligible for a competition, a sole-source award, or a Department of Veterans Affairs opportunity. But certification does not replace market research, proposal discipline, or contract performance. Treat it as an access credential, then build the operating system around it.

## What SDVOSB certification unlocks

A Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business (SDVOSB) is generally at least 51% owned and controlled by one or more service-disabled veterans. The qualifying veteran must manage the business and hold the highest officer role when the company is organized as a corporation. Current eligibility and verification rules should always be confirmed with the Small Business Administration and the VA, because program requirements and systems change.

Once verified, an SDVOSB may compete for set-asides reserved for this community. Agencies also have authority to make certain sole-source awards when statutory conditions are met, including a qualified firm, a fair and reasonable price, and circumstances where a competitive award is not appropriate. Certification can improve your position in market research and subcontracting conversations, but it is never a guarantee of an award.

Your registration should tell one consistent story. The qualifying veteran's ownership documents, voting rights, bank authority, resume, and day-to-day decisions should match what appears in SAM.gov, your VA profile, and your capability statement. Inconsistent control is a common reason for questions or rejection.

## Finding service-disabled veteran federal contracts

Start in SAM.gov Contract Opportunities with your NAICS codes, core service terms, place of performance, and a realistic notice date. Filter for SDVOSB and VOSB set-asides, but also search total small-business opportunities where your size standard fits. Save several narrow searches rather than one broad search that produces noise. Search both the service you provide and the outcome agencies buy; an agency may describe cybersecurity, for example, as security operations, network defense, or information assurance.

Read notices before investing proposal hours. Sources Sought and Requests for Information are market research, not offers, but they are useful signals. Respond accurately with your business size, certification status, capabilities, relevant experience, and questions. Presolicitations can reveal an incumbent, draft scope, industry day, and anticipated contract vehicle. When the solicitation arrives, download every attachment, amendment, Q&A, and referenced document.

Create a simple qualification record for each opportunity: agency and office, NAICS and size standard, set-aside type, contract vehicle, place of performance, security or clearance requirements, incumbent, deadline, and a one-sentence fit assessment. Reject opportunities quickly when you lack a required vehicle, clearance, geographic presence, or past performance. A small, well-qualified pipeline is more valuable than a long list of deadlines.

## Pay special attention to VA opportunities

The Department of Veterans Affairs is a major customer for veteran-owned firms. Look for opportunities through SAM.gov and VA procurement resources, and learn the terminology used by the specific medical center or contracting office you want to serve. Track forecast notices and sources-sought announcements early. The VA's requirements may include health-care-specific privacy, credentialing, quality, facility access, or supply-chain obligations that are easy to miss in a high-level summary.

Do not assume that being SDVOSB alone qualifies you for every VA procurement. Check whether the notice is limited to verified SDVOSBs, VOSBs, or another category, and confirm the exact ownership and verification language. Review delivery locations, ordering procedures, contract vehicles, and socioeconomic goals. A strong relationship with a small-business specialist can help you understand buying patterns, but all proposal claims must be supported and all formal instructions still control.

## VETS-4212 reporting is an ongoing responsibility

VETS-4212 is an annual report for federal contractors and subcontractors covered by the Vietnam Era Veterans' Readjustment Assistance Act (VEVRAA). It collects workforce and hiring data related to protected veterans. It is separate from SDVOSB certification, but veteran-focused contractors frequently encounter it as they grow their federal business.

Keep a calendar for the annual reporting window and determine whether your company and contracts are covered. Gather employee data from reliable HR records, identify veteran categories carefully, and retain documentation supporting your submission. Do not guess at classifications or leave reporting until the last week. Coordinate the report with the person responsible for affirmative-action and federal contractor compliance, and review current Department of Labor instructions for deadlines and covered entities.

## Common SDVOSB mistakes

- Treating certification as a substitute for a capability statement, relevant past performance, and a price strategy.
- Letting a non-qualifying partner make operational decisions while the veteran appears to control the company on paper.
- Missing an amendment, Q&A, page limit, or required representation because the team downloaded only the first notice.
- Claiming eligibility without checking the solicitation's NAICS size standard, place of performance, vehicle, or clearance requirement.
- Ignoring VETS-4212, annual registrations, contract reporting, or changing ownership and control information.
- Pursuing every veteran set-aside instead of selecting agencies and requirements where your team can perform profitably.
